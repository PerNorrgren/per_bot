Meet the hijacker
A fire alarm that won't find its reset.
Somewhere deep in your brain — lower than thought, lower than memory — a small cluster of cells has one job: sound the alarm. In real danger, it saves your life. The problem is what happens when it's been firing for so long, on so little real evidence, that it can't find its off switch.
The problem was never the person. The problem was a system that had been hijacked.
You're not imagining it

Each one has a different job. Together — without coordinating, without even knowing about each other — they keep the alarm going in ways one villain alone never could.

The Bouncer Who Isn't There

Why it keeps not working — until it does
Five minutes, five times a day.
Not one long session a week — five brief returns, every day. The brainstem's brake needs repeated windows of reduced alarm to recover, and thirty-five short windows a week are not remotely the same as one long one. Add real sleep, the only shift the brain's own cleaning crew ever gets, and the setting genuinely starts to change.
Five minutes of not monitoring. After six years of therapy and eight months of this work.
The hero was never missing
Your nervous system was built to settle.
It's still in there.
A brake. A cleaning crew. A body-map. A belonging system. These aren't things you have to acquire — they're things you already have, just hijacked by a loop that learned, for very real reasons, to run this way. Every time you press your heels into the floor, you're not creating something new. You're giving a system that's been waiting the signal it needed to respond.
