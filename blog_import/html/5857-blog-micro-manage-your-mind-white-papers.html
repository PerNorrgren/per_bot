Can we influence emotional states by working with the body and nervous system directly?

We often assume that our emotions arise passively in response to life’s events. A difficult conversation, an unexpected setback, or a moment of beauty appears to trigger a wave of feeling that washes over us — sometimes calming, sometimes overwhelming. But what if we’ve only been addressing one half of the equation? What if there are ways to engage with the source of emotion itself — to modulate or recreate the underlying nervous system signals that generate our emotional landscape?

Two new white papers, available now as part of the Deeper Mindfulness Research Series, explore this possibility with depth, scientific rigour, and grounded curiosity.

Read the white papers HERE

Together, they offer a compelling insight into how ancient neural structures and subtle muscular processes play a vital role in the way we feel, think, and respond — and how we might begin to work with these systems directly to create lasting transformation.

Why look below the surface of emotional experience?

Many therapeutic, contemplative, and behavioural approaches to mental health and wellbeing — from psychotherapy to mindfulness-based interventions — work with the outcomes of emotion. We learn to regulate, reframe, or observe our emotional states. We gain tools to reduce reactivity or increase compassion. These are invaluable.

But beneath the surface of conscious thought, our primitive nervous system — including the brainstem, limbic system, and autonomic circuits — is continually sending signals that set the stage for emotion to arise. These signals influence whether we feel safe or threatened, open or guarded, calm or agitated.

The first white paper, Recreating Primitive Nervous System Signals: A Neuroscientific Review of Artificial Modulation Techniques, explores how contemporary neuroscience is beginning to study, and in some cases artificially recreate, these signals through a variety of technological and physiological means.

This is not science fiction. It is a rapidly developing field, and its implications are profound.

What are primitive emotional signals, and why do they matter?

Primitive emotional signals originate in the oldest parts of the brain — those evolutionarily shaped to help us survive. These include instinctual patterns such as:
- Fight or flight responses to threat
- Orienting toward reward or safety
- Freezing in uncertainty
- Mobilising energy for connection or escape

These are not “bad” or “negative” states. In fact, they form the core physiological backdrop of all emotional life. But when the system becomes dysregulated — as in chronic anxiety, trauma, or emotional numbness — these signals can fire in ways that are unhelpful, unconscious, and difficult to influence.

Real-life example:

Sarah, a mindfulness practitioner and therapist, noticed that even with years of training, she couldn’t stop the surge of panic she felt when confronted by conflict. It felt like her body had already made the decision — to freeze or flee — before her mind caught up. Reading the first white paper helped her realise that her reaction wasn’t a failure of mindfulness but a primitive signalling issue. This reframed her approach from “trying harder” to self-regulate, to becoming curious about how her nervous system was communicating in those moments.

What does the first white paper explore?

In Recreating Primitive Nervous System Signals, the paper maps the cutting-edge landscape of interventions that attempt to influence the nervous system from the bottom up. These include:
- Neurostimulation techniques such as deep brain stimulation (DBS), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), and vagus nerve stimulation (VNS)
- Pharmacological agents that affect neuromodulators like dopamine, serotonin, and norepinephrine
- Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and neurofeedback systems that monitor and respond to brain activity in real time
- Computational models attempting to simulate emotional states based on neural circuit architecture

The paper offers a clear, accessible summary of where these technologies are currently effective, where they fall short, and what ethical considerations arise as we move toward artificially inducing feeling states.

Critically, it concludes that while progress is being made, there is not yet a non-invasive, precise method for recreating these signals authentically. Still, this growing field offers valuable insights into how our most foundational emotional signals arise and how we might one day work with them directly.

Can the body itself be used to shift emotion?

This question leads us directly to the second white paper, The Influence of Subtle Muscle Activity on Emotional and Cognitive Nervous Systems. While the first paper surveys technological interventions, the second looks to the physiological body — particularly the micro-movements and subtle contractions that occur in muscle tissue, often below conscious awareness.

It turns out that how we hold ourselves — literally — changes how we feel.

From the tightness of the jaw to the gentle lift of the eyebrows, from breath-related diaphragm shifts to quiet tensing in the shoulders, these subtle activities send constant signals to the brain about the internal and external environment.

Why do micro-movements matter?

Traditional systems of mindfulness, yoga, and even indigenous rituals have long understood that the body’s posture and expression can influence the mind. But what has often been dismissed as “posture work” or “relaxation techniques” is now being seen through a more nuanced lens: interoception, proprioception, and facial feedback mechanisms all involve a dialogue between muscle tone and emotional state.

The second paper explores this dialogue with scientific precision and contemplative openness.

Real-life example:

John, a retired teacher, began experimenting with a simple practice mentioned in the paper: gently softening the muscles around the eyes and forehead during moments of emotional stress. To his surprise, this small action shifted his internal state almost immediately — his mind became less critical, his body more grounded. “I used to try to change my thoughts,” he said. “Now I start with changing my face, just a little. It’s like I’m signalling to myself that I’m safe.”

What’s in the second white paper?

This paper offers an overview of:
- How facial and postural micro-contractions influence brain circuits related to emotion, memory, and attention
- The physiological foundations of embodied emotion regulation
- Recent studies on electromyography (EMG) and muscle-spindle signalling
- How intentional engagement with micro-movements can be used therapeutically

It also proposes a framework for deliberate micro-movement interventions, particularly useful for people who find traditional mindfulness or top-down cognitive approaches difficult to access during acute emotional episodes.

- Individuals with a deep interest in how emotion arises and how it can be transformed

Even if you are not a specialist, the papers are written with clarity, elegance, and practical curiosity. You’ll come away with new language, new insights, and new options.

What can I do with this knowledge in daily life?

The first step is to see your body not just as a receiver of emotional states, but as a generator of emotional context.

You might start noticing how the smallest tension in your face shifts your mood, or how releasing your jaw while breathing opens your attention.

You might begin to realise that your emotional patterns are not personal failures, but primitive neural habits, and that working with them requires subtlety, patience, and new tools.

And you might find that combining technological insight with embodied curiosity opens a new frontier of inner work.
