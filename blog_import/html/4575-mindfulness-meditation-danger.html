Ready to untangle the mysteries of your mind while staying as cool as a morning jog in the park? Before you throw meditation into the “too intense” category, consider this: meditation is like running, but not every session needs to be an ultra-marathon. Imagine telling someone to stop running because a few marathoners sprained their ankles. This is in my simple view essentially the argument behind recent calls for a “warning label” on meditation. I think it’s important to acknowledge that not all experiences with meditation are rainbows and enlightenment, but we must be careful not to throw the proverbial baby out with the bathwater. Let’s untangle this knot, layer by layer, much like a seasoned meditator unravels the complexities of their mind.

The Nature of Meditation: A Revealer, Not a Creator

Meditation is often misunderstood as a tool to induce inner peace. That’s like assuming running only makes you fit without considering how sore muscles can get. Meditation—especially mindfulness practices—acts as a mirror, reflecting back whatever is already present. Just as a gentle jog might reveal poor cardio health or tight muscles you didn’t realise you had, mindfulness meditation reveals thoughts, emotions, and patterns lurking under your everyday distractions.

Take Mary, for example, a 42-year-old professional struggling with anxiety. She began meditating to find peace but ended up confronting buried memories of unresolved trauma. Her anxiety spiked, leaving her more agitated after each session. Was meditation to blame? Not exactly. Meditation simply revealed what was already there, shining a light on deep-rooted issues. A slow jog doesn’t cause pain—it uncovers the state of your body. Similarly, meditation exposes your mental terrain, warts and all.

A Little Brain Science

In my simple view, neuroscience supports this notion. Studies have shown that regular mindfulness practice activates the prefrontal cortex, the area of the brain responsible for higher-order thinking, emotional regulation, and self-awareness. But here’s the rub: it also reduces activity in the amygdala, the brain’s fear centre. For someone with unresolved trauma or anxiety, this can feel like shaking up a snow globe—everything once settled is now swirling around.

While this can be unsettling, it’s not inherently harmful. In fact, with the right support, it can be profoundly healing. In the case of John, who participated in an 8-week Mindfulness For Life (MBCT-L program, the initial anxiety gave way to emotional breakthroughs. He realised he had spent years suppressing feelings of grief. Through mindfulness, he finally faced them head-on. The MBCT-L structure, akin to a well-balanced jogging routine, provided the necessary support and pacing to deal with these issues safely.

The Gentle Jog: Structured Mindfulness Practices

Just as a beginner wouldn’t start running with an ultra-marathon, mindfulness practices like MBCT-L are designed as a gentle jog through your mind. Structured, balanced, and professional programs, such as MBCT-L, Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) and Mindfulness Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), offer a gradual introduction to meditation. Over 8 weeks, participants are guided through practices designed to increase self-awareness and emotional regulation, without overloading the system.

The UK school study. This large-scale study mentioned in recent articles showed that mindfulness had no positive effect on children. But here’s the catch: many of the children didn’t actually engage in the practice. It’s a bit like handing out running shoes but never getting the kids off the couch. Structured programs rely on regular participation and proper guidance—something that’s difficult to ensure in a broad study.

Real-world examples tell a different story. Paul, a 36-year-old engineer, attended an MBCT-L program to manage work stress. Initially sceptical, he found the structured, incremental approach easy to follow. By Week 6, he noticed a significant reduction in stress and an increase in patience and clarity. Meditation, like jogging, worked for him because it was approached with the right tools and in the right conditions.

The Ultra-Marathon: Extreme Practices and Their Dangers

On the other end of the spectrum, we have the ultra-marathon of meditation practices—prolonged silent retreats, ego-dissolving techniques, and unregulated practices that can overwhelm the psyche. Just as running an ultra-marathon without training can cause serious injury, intensive meditation without proper guidance can lead to psychological distress. In rare cases, meditation has been linked to dissociation, depersonalisation, or psychotic breaks—particularly in individuals with underlying vulnerabilities such as trauma or psychosis.

Jessica learned this the hard way. She joined a week-long silent retreat with no prior meditation experience. By Day 3, she felt disoriented, anxious, and disconnected from reality. The intensity of the retreat, combined with sensory deprivation, left her emotionally raw. It’s not that meditation itself caused these issues—it’s the intensity and lack of preparation that triggered the reaction. Like jumping from a couch to an ultra-marathon, it was too much, too soon.

Practical Advice: How to Approach Meditation Safely

Just as we don’t stop people from running because a few have sprained their ankles, we shouldn’t scare people away from meditation. The key is preparation, pacing, and support.

1.  Start Slow: If you’re new to meditation, think of it like a light jog. Begin with a structured program like MBSR or MBCT, where trained professionals can guide you through the process. This ensures you don’t overload yourself or push too hard too soon.

2.  Get Support: Just as you’d consult a trainer before a marathon, talk to a mental health professional or meditation teacher, especially if you have pre-existing mental health conditions. They can help tailor your practice to your needs.

3.  Practice Regularly: Like running, meditation benefits from consistency. Engage with the practice daily, even if it’s only for a few minutes. Over time, you’ll build the mental “muscles” to handle deeper introspection.

4.  Listen to Your Body (and Mind): If a practice feels too intense, it probably is. Scale back and find a pace that works for you. There’s no rush to achieve enlightenment.

A Balanced Perspective: Revealer and Catalyst

In conclusion, meditation, like running, is a tool that, when used properly, can enhance mental well-being. It serves as both a revealer, showing us what lies beneath the surface, and a catalyst, offering the opportunity to process and heal what emerges. Just as a jogger might feel sore after their first run, a meditator might feel emotional discomfort as buried issues come to light. But with the right guidance and pacing, both lead to growth and transformation.

So, before we slap a warning label on meditation, let’s consider the context. Meditation isn’t dangerous—it’s powerful. And like all powerful tools, it must be used with care and respect. Rather than fearing the practice, we should educate people about how to approach it responsibly. After all, as Gandhi might say, “In a gentle way, you can shake the world”—or in this case, your mind.
