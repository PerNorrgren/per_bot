Wellbeing and Selfcare with Feeling Tone

There you are, sitting at your favourite café, about to take a bite of that gooey chocolate brownie. Normally, it’s heaven in your mouth. But today? You’ve just had the world’s biggest lunch. As the brownie approaches your lips, you realise with a sinking feeling… it’s just not hitting the same. Is it the brownie? Nope. It’s you. Or rather, how you’re meeting the brownie in that moment.

In the vast landscape of human experience, we like to think we know how things will make us feel. After all, chocolate is always delicious, right? Wrong. The feeling tone—the emotional resonance—of anything we encounter doesn’t exist in the object itself. Instead, it arises from how that object meets your body and mind. How you meet the chocolate. How you meet that annoying red light, or the chirping birds in the morning. Feeling tones aren’t always what you expect, and understanding this can lead to profound shifts in how we live our daily lives.

The Neuroscience of Feeling Tone: The Brain’s Role

Neuroscientifically speaking, feeling tones are created in the moment, depending on your internal state and external stimuli. The brain’s limbic system, particularly the amygdala, is responsible for tagging experiences with emotions. However, what most people don’t realise is that the amygdala is a highly contextual little beast. If you’re stressed, tired, or just had an argument, the chocolate brownie might feel like an insult to your overstuffed stomach rather than a reward for your hard day.

In terms of behaviour, feeling tone governs much of our reactive tendencies. A sudden sound in a quiet room might provoke irritation if you’re already tense, but it could just as easily feel like a welcome distraction if you were bored. This variance happens because your mind is always scanning your bodily state, integrating information from your autonomic nervous system. How much energy do you have? Are you hungry, tired, anxious? Your mind then uses these signals to determine how it should react to external objects. No wonder a traffic light can feel like either an enemy or a friend, depending on whether you’re running late or ridiculously early.

How It Affects Our Daily Lives

So what does this mean for our daily lives? It means that what you think you know about how things will make you feel is often wrong. Ever bought a ticket to a highly anticipated concert, only to find that, when the day comes, you’re not in the mood at all? Or, have you ever dreaded a social event, only to end up having a great time? That’s feeling tone at work, reminding you that your current emotional state influences how you perceive the world.

Take Anna, for example. She loves her morning run through the park. The air is fresh, the birds are chirping, and normally she feels invigorated and alive. But one morning, after a bad night’s sleep and an argument with her partner, the same run felt exhausting. The birds seemed obnoxious, the fresh air too cold, and her usual route felt like an uphill battle. Was it the run? Of course not. It was how Anna was meeting the run in that moment.

Another example: Tom hates grocery shopping. He dreads the lines, the crowds, the endless decisions about which brand of pasta sauce to buy. But one Saturday, after an unexpected promotion at work, he found himself humming while picking up apples. The crowds didn’t bother him. In fact, he noticed how pleasant it was to be out and about. Same supermarket, different feeling tone.

The feeling tone—whether something feels pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral—isn’t baked into the experience itself. It’s all about your state of being in the moment, and how that experience is interacting with your body and mind.

Neuroscience Behind the Scenes: The Body-Mind Connection

So, what’s happening beneath the surface when these shifts occur? At the neural level, sensory information from the environment is processed first by your brainstem and limbic system. These ancient brain regions are responsible for your basic survival instincts—should you flee, fight, or stay? This is where feeling tone gets its first cue. If you’re already agitated, a traffic light might feel like a personal affront because your amygdala is primed to view the world through a lens of threat. If you’re calm and relaxed, the same light could offer a welcome pause.

But here’s where it gets interesting: once the feeling tone is set, it can shape your perception of the event. This is called affective priming. For example, if you already feel anxious about being late, every little inconvenience (like that red light) will seem more frustrating. On the other hand, if you’re in a positive or neutral state, you’re more likely to meet the same event with equanimity.

The prefrontal cortex, which governs decision-making and emotional regulation, has the ability to override these instinctive reactions—if we are mindful enough. It’s here that practices like meditation come into play, allowing us to notice our automatic responses and intentionally change how we meet each moment.

Why It Matters and How to Use It

Now that we know how feeling tone arises, we can begin to shift our relationship to it. Rather than assuming the chocolate will always be delicious or the traffic light will always be a pain, we can start observing how we are in the moment. This opens up the possibility of choosing how we respond. We can meet the world with curiosity rather than expectation, seeing how our internal state affects the way we perceive external objects.

Here’s a simple example from Claire’s life. Claire used to think she hated rainy days. Whenever the skies opened up, she’d grumble and complain, convinced it ruined her mood. But after learning about feeling tone, she started to notice how, on some rainy days, she actually felt calm and cosy. On others, she felt restless and agitated. The rain wasn’t the problem—it was how she met the rain on any given day.

Once we recognise that the feeling tone is a moving target, we can stop blaming the chocolate, the traffic, or the rain for how we feel. Instead, we can tune into our internal state and recognise what’s driving our emotional reactions.

How to Deal with Shifting Feeling Tones

Alright, so you’ve gotten the gist: your feeling tone is a sneaky shape-shifter, and it’s not always what you expect. But how do you work with this in real life?

Here’s a straightforward guide to dealing with this phenomenon:

1.  Pause and Check In: Before or when you realise that you are reacting to an experience, pause for a moment. Ask yourself, “How am I meeting this?” Notice your internal state.

2.  Don’t Assume: Just because something was pleasant (or unpleasant) in the past doesn’t mean it will be the same today. Stay open to the possibility of a different experience.

3.  Cultivate Curiosity: Treat each moment like an experiment. “Hmm, how is my body reacting to this now?” By staying curious, you can avoid getting stuck in old patterns of reactivity.

4.  Change the Internal, Not the External: Instead of trying to fix or change the situation (like scarfing down chocolate hoping it will magically taste good), try shifting your internal state through a deep breath exhaling slowly, smiling, acknowledging the reaction.

5.  Be Kind to Yourself: If you’re having a rough day and nothing seems to feel right, acknowledge it. Your feeling tone isn’t static, and sometimes you just need to give yourself permission to feel what you’re feeling without forcing it to change. Simply say "It is human to feel like this".

Wrapping It Up with a Smile

Life is full of unexpected moments, and the feeling tone is the ultimate reminder that we can’t always predict how things will land. But instead of getting frustrated, we can see it as a playful mystery. Today, chocolate might be too sweet. Tomorrow, it could be bliss. The traffic light could be an annoyance now, but later, it might be the perfect opportunity to catch your breath.

Understanding this dance between internal and external can turn life into an ongoing exploration. Each moment becomes an opportunity to meet whatever comes your way with a little more curiosity and a little less judgment.

In short, it’s not about changing the chocolate—it’s about changing how you meet it.
