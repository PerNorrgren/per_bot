What if the way you feel isn’t the truth—but just your brain’s best guess?

We tend to think of emotions as real, solid, and honest—like the body’s way of telling us what’s going on. But modern neuroscience tells us something much more curious: emotions aren’t hardwired responses. They’re predictions. Your brain is constantly guessing what’s happening inside and around you, then constructing an emotion to fit.

This means your feelings are less like a camera and more like Photoshop: filtered, layered, and informed by what came before.

And here’s the kicker: if the brain never gets contradictory evidence—never receives new input—it keeps repeating the same emotional responses. That’s how we get stuck. That’s what makes healing so elusive for so many. And that’s exactly why MMPM (multi-modal present moment) is a game-changer.

So, let’s explore how our beautifully predictive brain can trip us up—and how we can use simple, embodied tools to gently teach it something new.

What is emotion, really?

To understand MMPM, we need to start with what an emotion actually is. According to leading researchers like Lisa Feldman Barrett, emotions are constructed—not triggered. The brain uses past experience, bodily signals (like heart rate or tension), and context (like the tone of someone’s voice) to predict what’s happening and generate an emotional response.

This is called predictive coding. It’s the brain’s way of saving energy: rather than processing every detail afresh, it draws on stored models to pre-guess the situation. It’s metabolically efficient, but also error-prone.

If you’ve ever walked into a room and felt like something was off—even though nothing had happened—that’s your predictive system at work. Sometimes it’s brilliant. Other times, it’s just recycling old stories.

How does emotion affect behaviour and bodily processes?

Once an emotion is constructed, it doesn't just live in your mind. It mobilises your whole body. Emotions shape:

  • Heart rate and breathing
  • Muscle tone and posture
  • Gut activity
  • Attention and perception
  • Impulse and action

When you feel anger, your body prepares to fight. When you feel shame, your body might collapse inward. The emotion isn’t separate from the sensation—it is the sensation, interpreted.

This loop between prediction and bodily reaction can become self-reinforcing. The brain predicts threat → the body braces → the brain reads this bracing as proof of threat → and round we go.

What is emotional entanglement?

Entanglement is what happens when you become fused with your emotional prediction. You don’t just have fear—you are afraid. You don’t just notice a thought—you are the thought.

This might sound dramatic, but it’s incredibly common. Most of us live much of our day inside these loops:

  • Shame spirals after a meeting
  • Anxiety before an email 
  • Irritation on repeat with a partner

In each case, we’re no longer observing experience—we’re merged with it. Mindfulness helps us notice this entanglement. But noticing alone is not enough.

Why isn’t mindfulness always enough?

Here’s the uncomfortable truth: just watching your reactions doesn’t always change them. You can sit with anxiety every day and still feel anxious the next time your boss emails you.

Why? Because the brain doesn’t update its models unless something unexpected happens. In neuroscience terms, this is called *prediction error*. It’s the mismatch between what the brain expects and what actually occurs.

So if the system predicts shame and you just observe it? No update. But if the system expects shame—and instead receives warmth, grounding, or breath? That’s a prediction error. That’s when learning occurs.

What does MMPM actually do?

MMPM stands for multi-modal present moment. It’s a fancy term for a simple thing: bringing in multiple sensory channels to ground yourself in what’s happening now.

We’re talking:

  • Physical touch (like placing your hand on your chest)
  • Breath (especially long, slow exhales)
  • Sound (your voice, a calming tone, ambient noise)
  • Movement (standing upright, shifting posture, smile, lifting eyebrows)
  • Vision (softening your gaze, orienting to the space)

When you apply these during an emotional reaction—while the prediction is forming—you create a contradiction. The system expected collapse. It got connection. That’s where change lives.

Can you give me a real-life example?

Let’s say you’ve just made a social mistake. You fumble your words in a meeting and instantly feel shame. The old pattern kicks in: heart races, mind races, face flushes.

Old loop: Shame → collapse → harsh self-talk → social withdrawal.

MMPM loop: Shame → hand to chest → long exhale → say aloud "I’m okay" → feel feet on the floor → lift gaze and smile.

The brainstem was expecting shutdown. Instead, it got safety cues. That’s a contradiction. That’s prediction error. And that’s how the emotional model begins to update.

Why does this matter for trauma and old patterns?

Trauma—whether big or small—often embeds emotional predictions deep in the primitive system. You don’t just remember what happened—you expect it to happen again. The nervous system doesn’t update easily.

MMPM works because it goes under cognition. It speaks to the same system that stored the original learning: the sensorimotor, survival-based brain.

When someone who’s always braced for rejection receives a smile and feels it—that’s an update. When someone who fears collapse finds their spine and breath in a moment of fear—that’s new data.

We can’t think our way out of trauma. But we can teach the system differently—if we do it in the language it understands.

How do people actually cultivate this?

You don’t need years of training or a guru on a mountain. You need curiosity, some basic skills, and a willingness to practice.

Here’s how real people use MMPM:

  • A teacher who touches her forearm and breathes before giving a difficult parent update.
  • A man who pauses mid-argument, steps outside, and lets his shoulders drop while orienting to the sky.
  • A teenager who puts a hand on his belly when anxiety floods his chest before a test.

These aren’t magic tricks. They’re simple sensory cues—applied in the right moment—that tell the brain: something new is happening.

What if I don’t feel anything when I try?

That’s okay. It’s common to feel disconnected at first. The predictive system is dominant—it takes time to override it. But you don’t need fireworks.

All you need is a tiny mismatch. A breath. A shift in posture. A tone of voice that contradicts the old narrative. The more "heart-felt" we can make the smile and tension realse the more effective it is.

Even if you feel 99% caught in the emotion, the 1% of new sensory input begins to carve a new path. Over time, these small contradictions accumulate. The system learns.

How is this different from coping techniques?

Coping often means managing symptoms: calming down, distracting yourself, breathing through it. MMPM is different.

You’re not trying to escape the feeling. You’re meeting it with new data. You’re saying: "Yes, this is happening—and also, here is a different way."

This is why it works so well with therapy, mindfulness, and relational work. It’s not a replacement. It’s the missing piece.

Is this scientifically backed?

Yes, this is grounded in established neuroscience:

Predictive coding (Friston, 2010): The brain constantly forecasts sensory input.
Emotion construction (Barrett, 2017): Emotions are predictions, not reflexes.
Interoception and body-based learning (Damasio, 1999; Porges, 2011): The body plays a key role in perception and emotional memory.
Prediction error as the mechanism of learning (Rescorla & Wagner, 1972): Only surprise updates the system.

MMPM integrates these principles into something people can actually use. It’s elegant, grounded, and deeply human.

How can I start using MMPM?

Don’t overthink it. The brain loves complexity. But the body needs simplicity.

Here’s your quickstart guide:

Do this:

1. Notice the moment you feel caught (shame, anxiety, collapse)
2. Add a new sensory cue (hand to chest, breath, sound, posture, smile)
3. Stay with the mismatch—let the body register something new

That’s it. Noticing + new input + patience = change.

What happens over time?

The more you do this, the more your system learns:

“Not every mistake means collapse.”
“I can stay present when I feel fear.”
“I don’t have to disappear to be safe.”

This isn’t about becoming superhuman. It’s about becoming more human—with choice, presence, and room to grow.

Over time, MMPM becomes second nature. You don’t just know that you’re safe—you feel it. And that’s what rewires the system.

What should I do next?

If this resonates, bring it into your world.

  • Try it in small moments today: one breath, one hand to the heart.
  • Teach it to your clients, your kids, your partner.
  • Join our community of explorers—where we unpack this work in real time, together.

You already have the tools. The question is: are you willing to surprise your nervous system?

Because that’s where healing lives—not in the stories of the past, but in the gentle contradictions of the present.
