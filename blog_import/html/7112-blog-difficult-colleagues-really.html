Why Your “Difficult” Colleagues Aren’t Who You Think They Are

— A quiet shift in perspective for the stressed professional

For years, I thought some people at work were simply… difficult.

You know the ones.

  • The colleague who meets every idea with sarcasm.
  • The manager who seems permanently negative.
  • The developer who rolls their eyes before you’ve even finished the question.
  • The architect who cuts you off mid-sentence.
  • The senior leader who feels cold, unreachable, almost allergic to warmth.

For most of my career, I assumed this was personality.
Or attitude.
Or ego.
Or some unfortunate “type” you just have to put up with in technical environments.

I never considered the possibility that I wasn’t meeting the person — only their armour.

It wasn’t until I began looking at emotional life through a different lens — the lens of the raw emotional circuits that shape human behaviour — that everything changed.

Because beneath the negativity and cynicism, beneath the sarcasm, beneath the dryness and defensiveness, something else was happening:

People weren’t being difficult.
They were being protected.

Behind the cutting humour was an adult whose early attachment system learned during the formative years:

“Don’t expect closeness — it won’t be safe.”
(PANIC/GRIEF under-activation)

Behind the constant tension was a nervous system saying:

“Stay guarded — something will go wrong.”
(FEAR over-activation)

Behind the flatness and lack of motivation was a SEEKING system that had learned:

“Trying doesn’t lead to reward — better not to start.”

Behind the rigidity, the snapping, the impatience:

“My boundaries were not respected in the past —
I must defend them now.”
(RAGE masked as sarcasm or control)

And behind the dismissiveness — always behind it — was SHAME.

Not the dramatic kind.
The quiet, corrosive kind:

“If I soften, I will be hurt again.”

Once I understood this, the people I had been frustrated with for decades suddenly looked different. I began to see not the difficult adult in front of me, but the younger version of them — the child who didn’t receive the steady care, emotional safety, or attunement they needed.

The patterns that irritate us now were shaped back then, long before they had a choice. And in that moment, the sharp edges of their behaviour made sense: they were never trying to be difficult; they were trying to survive with the emotional tools they were given.

But because I finally saw what was driving it.

The professional world only amplifies these patterns.

  • Emotional suppression is rewarded.
  • Distance is mistaken for competence.
  • Sarcasm passes as intelligence.
  • Perfectionism becomes survival.
  • Hyper-independence is celebrated.
  • And vulnerability… well, that never makes the performance review.

So the people I saw as cynical, combative, or cold were often:

  • emotionally overloaded
  • under-supported
  • terrified of looking incompetent
  • afraid of being wrong
  • hiding exhaustion behind sharpness
  • protecting themselves from shame
  • living in a culture that never made room for emotion

They weren’t judging me.
They were defending themselves.

And somehow, understanding that released something heavy in me — a burden I had carried far too long.

Because when behaviour stops feeling personal, it stops feeling threatening.

And when you can see the emotional circuitry under someone’s reactions, compassion becomes the most natural thing in the world.

No, this doesn’t excuse poor behaviour.
But it explains it.
And explanation is often the first step toward freedom — for them, and for you.

What I wish I had known years ago is simple:

Most “difficult” people are

  • Wounded, not wilful.
  • Protected, not hostile.
  • Armoured, not arrogant.

And once you see that, the entire emotional landscape of your working life changes.
