    How does poetry speak to the soul in a time of uncertainty?

    Poetry, in these disoriented times, is not a luxury. It is a lifeline. As I observe the trembling foundations of the world we inhabit—from the erratic climate to the frayed fabric of our social systems—I find that poetry serves not as an escape, but as a mirror. It reflects what we dare not say aloud. It articulates grief, hope, and longing in a way that ordinary speech cannot. And in this act, it soothes and transforms.

    In a recent article from the Greater Good Science Center titled How Poetry Changes You and Your Brain, the authors draw upon emerging research that validates what some of us have intuited all our lives: poetry changes us, not merely in mood but in structure. It shifts the neural pathways. It sharpens empathy. It bonds us to others across the divide of culture and circumstance.

    What happens to the mind when we read or write poetry?

    Neuroscientists are beginning to trace the contours of the poetic mind. When we read or write poetry, parts of the brain linked with emotion, memory, and even motor control light up. The Greater Good article reports that poetry stimulates the default mode network, a system associated with introspection and self-reflection. Poetry, then, is not simply a linguistic activity; it is a physiological reorganisation. The mind enters a state of openness, a quiet alertness.

    On a grey morning in London, shortly after a protest had turned violent. The city was hushed with collective fatigue. On the train, a man  quietly read Derek Walcott’s Love After Love. When asked why that poem. He said, “Because it helps me remember who I am, beyond the noise.” Poetry anchors us, even when the world is unmoored.

    Can poetry be a form of therapy?

    It already is. In hospices, in refugee shelters, in prisons and youth centres, poetry circles are emerging not as art projects, but as mental health interventions. The article I cited earlier describes how poetry groups, particularly during the isolation of the COVID-19 pandemic, became lifelines for emotional survival.

    There is a story I hold dear. A nurse, battling burnout in a hospital in Marseille, began writing a single stanza each night before sleeping. Her poems were not polished. They were raw. But they became a container for her exhaustion, a place to lay down what she could not say in meetings or therapy. When she shared them with her team, the culture of the ward shifted. Suddenly, even the most stoic colleagues found language for their pain.

    Why does poetry increase empathy?

    Because it forces us into another person’s rhythm. Not only their thoughts, but the cadence of their breath, the tension in their pauses. Poetry, by design, slows us down. It requires patience and attention. And this pace is radically different from the scrolling, swiping, data-saturated world we live in.

    Reading poetry, especially from voices different to our own, dissolves boundaries. A poem by a Syrian mother can collapse the imagined space between her experience and ours. A verse penned by someone grieving in Tokyo may help an accountant in Leeds cry for the first time in years. Poetry operates not just in the intellect but in the body—the body that recognises grief, longing, joy, and hope in any tongue.

    Can poetry foster resilience?

    Resilience is not stoicism. It is not the absence of pain but the capacity to hold it without collapse. Poetry is one of the few tools we have that cultivates this quality. It builds what psychologists call “affective tolerance”—the ability to feel deeply without being overwhelmed.

    In therapy settings, poems are now being used to help trauma survivors regulate their emotional states. A facilitator may invite a participant to read a few lines aloud, and then reflect on the imagery. This creates a buffer between them and their emotional triggers, allowing integration rather than reactivity.

    I heard of a war correspondent, that used to carry a copy of Rainer Maria Rilke’s Book of Hours in his rucksack. Not for spiritual reasons, but for sanity. After witnessing unspeakable violence, he would read the poems aloud to himself before sleeping. He said the words helped put the world back into shape, even when logic failed to do so.

    How can poetry help us face the climate crisis?

    To live in the age of climate collapse is to carry a quiet dread. The facts are staggering and often paralysing. But poetry does not speak in statistics. It speaks in the language of water, fire, wind. It re-humanises what news reports dehumanise.

    Consider the work of poets like Camille T. Dungy or Alice Oswald, who write about ecosystems not as abstract data but as kin. Oswald’s Dart turns a river into a living presence. When we read such work, we are no longer simply environmentalists; we are lovers of the earth. And love, as we know, mobilises action far more effectively than fear.

    In the west, where climate discourse often alternates between denial and despair, poetry offers a third path: intimacy. It draws us into relationship with the more-than-human world. And in that relationship, we begin to care again.

    Is there still space for poetry in a distracted world?

    More than ever. Precisely because our attention is under siege. In a culture driven by algorithms and alerts, poetry is one of the few forms of language that cannot be optimised. It resists summarisation. It demands presence.

    I saw a person that spoke at a conference where executives from a tech firm discussed the crisis of attention among their staff. One woman, a senior manager, confessed that she had started reading haiku in the mornings before logging on. She found the brevity and depth of the form helped her “return to herself” after a night of restless scrolling. She began emailing a poem each week to her team. The practice caught on.

    Can we write our way through grief and loss?

    We must. Loss is no longer rare. It is everywhere. People are grieving not only loved ones, but identities, certainties, futures they thought were secure. Poetry gives shape to that grief. It allows mourning to be communal, even in solitude.

    There is a poem by Marie Howe, What the Living Do, that has found its way into countless memorial services and hospital rooms. In it, she writes not about death, but about the banal, stubborn acts of living after someone is gone: buying milk, folding laundry, forgetting. These poems do not offer solutions. They offer companionship. And sometimes that is more needed.

    During lockdown, people hosted online writing circles for friends scattered across continents. Each week they would write to a prompt and read aloud. One man in his seventies, recently widowed, wrote about his morning cup of coffee in such detail it brought the room to tears. It was not the coffee, of course. It was everything unsaid. Poetry allows the unsayable to speak.

    Where does poetry belong now?

    It belongs in boardrooms and bedrooms, on street walls and in subways, in government policies and in gardens. It belongs anywhere humans seek to make sense of their existence.

    As Indira Gandhi once spoke of the power of the word as greater than the sword. I believe this is so, though the world has changed. In the fragmented landscape of the modern West, poetry can be a stitching thread. It does not pretend to be a cure. But it is a salve. A spark. A still point in a turning world.

    Let us not wait for poetry to be prescribed. Let us live by it. Read it aloud in the kitchen. Pin it to the bathroom mirror. Send it to a friend instead of a text. And when the world becomes unrecognisable again—as it surely will—let us write the verses that remember us to ourselves.
