What if your reactivity isn’t really “you”?

You’re standing in the kitchen. Your partner says something—perhaps a little sharp, or just… off. Suddenly, your chest tightens. Your jaw locks. There it is again—that cold rush through your spine, the defensive bark that leaps out, or the cold silence that creeps in. You’ve promised yourself not to react like this again. But here you are.

Could it be that something deeper, older, is being touched?

What if these emotional storms aren’t flaws in your personality, but echoes of past wounds—ones that never had a chance to heal properly?

How do emotional wounds from the past remain active?

When a child or adolescent experiences emotional trauma—social rejection, neglect, humiliation, fear, or abandonment—the brain and nervous system take careful note. The amygdala (our threat detector) becomes hyper-tuned to social danger. The hippocampus (our memory and meaning-maker) records the context, feelings, and unmet needs. But in adolescence, the prefrontal cortex (the part that reflects, regulates and reasons) isn’t fully developed yet.

Without proper processing—co-regulation with an attuned adult, reflective conversations, naming and understanding the feelings—the system essentially freezes. These unprocessed loops lie dormant, quietly nested in the body, until something in adulthood sounds a similar alarm.

This isn’t psychological theory—it’s neurobiology.

Why do these patterns resurface in adult relationships?

Because relationships are the emotional mirrors of our developmental past.

Imagine this: a woman in her mid-thirties, successful in her career, begins to notice her irritation with her partner increasing. His casual comments feel like criticism. She finds herself scanning for signs that he might be withdrawing. She doesn’t understand why she can’t let things go.

What’s happening? Her nervous system, wired during adolescence for hypersensitivity to rejection or invalidation, is detecting echoes of old pain. The partner isn’t the problem—he’s the trigger.

So why can’t we just think our way out of it?

Because these responses are embodied. They live below language. When your nervous system senses a threat—real or remembered—it doesn’t check with your prefrontal cortex before it tightens your chest and triggers an emotional cascade. This is the work of the autonomic nervous system, particularly the limbic circuits shaped long before you had the words to name your pain.

Repairing this isn’t about “thinking positively”. It’s about going inward, slowly, safely, with presence.

What does MMPM have to do with healing?

MMPM—Micro-Move Predictive Mindfulness—is not just a toolkit for managing reactivity. It’s a door into the deeper landscape of your internal world. Because it works in micro-movements, it doesn’t flood the system. It builds tolerance for staying in the uncomfortable moment without needing to fix, fight, flee or freeze.

By becoming aware of a felt sense (that clench in your belly, that burn behind your eyes) and responding not with a story, but with space, we begin to unwind the loops. MMPM allows us to meet our experience before it takes us hostage.

Can this really change something that happened decades ago?

Yes—and here’s why.

The brain is plastic. It remodels in response to attention and experience. Every time you catch the impulse to defend or withdraw, and instead stay present with compassion—even if only for a moment—you are rewiring. Every pause, every breath you give to the ache instead of the argument, sends a signal: It’s safe now.

The past may echo, but it doesn’t have to rule.

Isn’t this the same as mindfulness or meditation?

Not quite. Traditional mindfulness often invites us to observe thoughts like clouds, let them drift by, and remain detached. MMPM is more embodied. It says: Go smaller. Go closer. Listen to the twitch of your gut, the whisper of your breath, the way your eyes tighten. It’s not about spacing out—it’s about tuning in.

This can be incredibly powerful for women whose emotional development was interrupted. Instead of trying to manage themselves through logic or avoidance, they learn to trust the signals of their own bodies.

How does it look in real life?

Let’s take Anna, a 41-year-old woman, in her third long-term relationship. She’s done therapy. She’s read the books. But every time her partner leaves the house without saying goodbye properly, she spirals. Anger. Tears. Accusation. Then shame.

Through MMPM, Anna began to notice the exact moment the sensation arose—just a tightening in her solar plexus. Instead of following it into narrative (“He doesn’t love me, he’s leaving, I’ll be alone”), she stayed with the sensation. It softened. She realised the root wasn’t about him—it was the child in her that never had a secure goodbye from her father.

This awareness didn’t fix everything overnight. But it gave her choices. Space. And slowly, the spiral began to change.

But what about her partner?

This is the part many approaches miss. The healing of emotional wounds doesn’t only live in the individual. It lives in relationship.

If a partner can learn to be a calm presence—not fixing, not defending, just being—it becomes medicine. Co-regulation is how the emotional system learns safety. One nervous system says to another: You’re okay. I’m here.

This doesn’t mean becoming a therapist. It means learning to stay.

What does the science say about this?

Interpersonal neurobiology shows that our brains are wired for connection. Dr. Dan Siegel calls it “mindsight”—our ability to perceive the inner world of ourselves and others. Trauma often disrupts this. But safe relationships—especially ones where emotions are met with empathy, not judgement—can heal it.

Studies show that mindfulness practices, especially body-based ones, reduce amygdala activation, strengthen prefrontal regulation, and improve vagal tone (our system for calming and connecting). Over time, these changes support more emotional balance, resilience, and even intimacy.

So how can you start repairing the system?

You don’t need a full retreat. You need three things, consistently:

1. Sense first, think later.
When you feel triggered, don’t go into your head. Go into your body. Ask: Where is this landing? What am I feeling? Can I just be here for a moment without acting?

2. Shrink the move.
Don’t try to change everything. Just notice one breath. One sensation. One urge to speak or shut down. Let that be enough.

3. Share from the inside.
Instead of saying, “You made me feel X,” try, “I noticed something tightening in me when that happened.” Bring the sensation, not the accusation.

These micro-shifts, repeated, can change your life.

What happens if nothing changes?

If you do this work and still feel stuck, that’s information. Sometimes deeper therapeutic support is needed—especially for those with complex trauma. But you’ll never waste the effort. Every micro-move is a signal to your system that you are learning to be safe, present, and sovereign.

Even your awareness of being stuck is a sign of awareness. That’s the first doorway.

What’s the cost of not doing this?

The longer unresolved patterns run the show, the more your life becomes shaped by old pain. Relationships become battlegrounds. Work becomes a place to prove worth. Parenting becomes a tangle of over-control or shutdown.

You deserve better.

So does your partner. So do your children.

Healing is not just possible. It is urgent.

where do we go from here?

This journey is about learning a new kind of strength—the strength to stay present. It’s not about controlling emotion, but befriending it. Not about fixing yourself, but meeting yourself.

And if you’re thinking, “Yes, but I’m not sure I can do this,”—that’s okay.

Start with one micro-move. One breath. One decision not to react, but to listen.

The work begins here.
